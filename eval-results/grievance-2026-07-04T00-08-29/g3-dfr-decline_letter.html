This review included: 1. **The grievance file**, including your written statement, the employer's discharge letter dated February 3, 2026, the Step 1 and Step 2 responses, and all correspondence between you and the union; 2. **The collective agreement**, particularly Article 7 (grievance procedure) and the provisions governing discipline and discharge; 3. **The investigation materials**, including the incident report from the forklift collision on February 3, 2026, witness statements from coworkers present in the warehouse at the time, the post-incident drug test results, and the employer's zero tolerance policy on impairment; 4. **Your employment record**, including your seniority date of January 10, 2022, the verbal warning issued in 2024 concerning a safety matter, and the written warning issued in 2025 concerning a near-miss incident; 5. **A legal opinion** obtained from experienced labour counsel specializing in arbitration, which assessed the merits of the grievance and the likelihood of success at arbitration; 6. **Comparable arbitration decisions** addressing discharge for positive post-incident drug tests, the evidentiary standards applied by arbitrators, and the remedies granted in similar cases; and 7. **A review by the bargaining unit grievance committee**, which met on [INSERT DATE] to consider the file and the legal opinion before making a recommendation to the executive. The union has given serious consideration to each of these points. However, after reviewing the evidence and obtaining legal advice, the union has concluded that the grievance faces significant obstacles at arbitration that make success unlikely. **The Evidence of Impairment.** The employer's case rests on the post-incident drug test, which returned a positive result for THC. You have consistently maintained that you were not impaired at work and that the test reflects only recreational use on your own time days before the incident. The legal opinion advises that while arbitrators have recognized the limitations of drug testing in proving workplace impairment, particularly for cannabis, the context of a workplace accident significantly strengthens the employer's position. The forklift collision caused damage to racking and product, and occurred during your shift. Arbitrators have upheld discipline, including discharge, where a positive post-incident test is combined with a safety incident, even where the grievor denies impairment. The union would bear the burden of proving that you were not impaired, and the available evidence does not provide a strong basis to meet that burden. No witnesses observed you before the collision in a manner that would support an inference of sobriety, and you did not seek an independent test immediately after the incident that might have provided contrary evidence. **The Employer's Policy.** You have argued that the zero tolerance policy is unreasonable and was not properly posted or communicated. The legal opinion notes that while policies must meet the test set out in *KVP Co. Ltd.*, [1965] O.L.A.A. No. 2, arbitrators have generally upheld zero tolerance policies concerning impairment at work, particularly in safety-sensitive environments such as warehouse operations involving forklift use. The evidence in the file indicates that the policy was included in the employee handbook you received during orientation in January 2022, was posted in the warehouse break room, and was the subject of a safety meeting in September 2025 that you attended, as reflected in the sign-in sheet. While you may not recall the specifics of the policy, the union's assessment is that an arbitrator would likely find the policy was sufficiently clear and brought to your attention. **Your Prior Discipline.** Your employment record includes a verbal warning in 2024 concerning a safety matter and a written warning in 2025 concerning a near-miss incident. You have argued that this prior discipline was minor, unrelated to impairment, and should not be held against you. The legal opinion advises that while the prior discipline does not directly relate to substance use, it does establish a pattern of safety concerns that an arbitrator may consider relevant in assessing the employment relationship and the appropriateness of the penalty. Arbitrators frequently consider the "culminating incident" doctrine, under which a final serious incident may justify discharge even where prior discipline addressed different matters, provided there is a common thread. Here, the common thread is workplace safety. **The Remedy.** Even if the union were able to establish that the discharge was excessive, the legal opinion cautions that reinstatement is far from certain. Arbitrators have broad remedial discretion, and in cases involving positive drug tests and safety incidents, reinstatement is often made conditional on the grievor completing a substance abuse assessment, agreeing to random testing, and accepting a significant suspension without pay. In some cases, arbitrators have substituted a lengthy suspension for discharge but have declined to order reinstatement where trust has been irreparably damaged. The union must consider not only the likelihood of success on the merits, but also the likelihood that the remedy, if any, would meet your needs. ## What the Union Weighed In making this decision, the union was required to weigh several factors: 1. **The merits of the grievance**, based on the evidence, the collective agreement, and the legal principles applied by arbitrators; 2. **The strength of the available evidence**, including the credibility of witnesses, the reliability of the drug test, and the absence of evidence directly contradicting the employer's case; 3. **The cost of arbitration**, which typically ranges from $15,000 to $30,000 or more when legal fees, arbitrator fees, and hearing time are considered, and which must be borne by the membership as a whole; 4. **The interests of the bargaining unit**, including the need to allocate limited resources to cases with reasonable prospects of success and to maintain the union's credibility before arbitrators by advancing grievances on solid ground; and 5. **Your interests as a member**, including the impact of the discharge on your livelihood and your desire to clear your name. The union does not take this decision lightly. You are a member of this union, and the union has a duty to represent you fairly. However, the duty of fair representation does not require the union to take every grievance to arbitration, regardless of merit. The union is entitled to make honest, considered judgments about the strength of a case, and to decline to advance grievances where the prospects of success are poor and the cost to the membership is significant. In this case, the legal opinion, the grievance committee, and the executive have all concluded that the grievance does not have reasonable prospects of success at arbitration. The decision was not made to save money, but rather because the merits do not justify the expenditure of resources that could be used to represent other members in stronger cases. ## Concerns You Have Raised In your communications with the union, you have raised several concerns about the union's representation.
