Union's Arbitration Brief

In the Matter of an Arbitration Between IAM Local 235 (the "Union") and Trillium Paper Products Inc (the "Employer")

Re: Grievance of Claude Boisvert (the "Grievor") - Discharge

Prepared by: Jordan Whitfield, Whitfield Labour Law, counsel for the Union.

This document is a draft prepared for review by union counsel and the responsible labour relations officer before use.

1. Overview

The Grievor, Claude Boisvert, is a Millwright in the Maintenance department at the Employer's Cornwall mill with approximately 18 years of continuous service dating from 17 March 2008. On 24 June 2026 the Employer alleged that the Grievor had falsified a lockout verification record on a single occasion, and by letter dated 26 June 2026 the Employer discharged him for "falsification of safety documentation." The discharge was imposed following a disciplinary meeting that lasted approximately four minutes, at which the Grievor had no union representation, and without any prior investigation. The Union seeks a declaration that the Employer lacked just cause, reinstatement of the Grievor with full back pay (estimated at $96,000), benefits, pension, and seniority, and interest.

2. The Parties and the Collective Agreement

The Union is the certified bargaining agent for the Grievor and other employees in the bargaining unit at the Employer's Cornwall mill. The parties are governed by the 2023–2026 Collective Agreement. The grievance and arbitration procedure is set out in Article 11, the time limits of which are stated to be mandatory. Article 5.02 is the just cause provision governing discipline and discharge. Its terms govern the standard by which the discharge in this matter must be assessed. (Union counsel to insert the verbatim text of Article 5.02 upon confirmation of the operative wording; the intake identifies it as the applicable just cause article.)

As a matter of settled Ontario law, the substantive standard of just cause and the remedial authority of the arbitrator to substitute a lesser penalty are engaged by Article 5.02 and by section 48(17) of the Labour Relations Act, 1995.

3. Statement of Facts

  1. The Grievor, Claude Boisvert, is employed as a Millwright in the Maintenance department at the Employer's Cornwall mill.
  2. The Grievor's seniority date is 17 March 2008, giving him approximately 18 years of continuous service as of the date of discharge.
  3. Over that lengthy period of service the Grievor's disciplinary record contains a single entry: one written warning issued in 2019 for late reporting, which was not grieved.
  4. The Collective Agreement provides for a sunset clause of 24 months, such that discipline older than that period is not available to support subsequent discipline.
  5. The 2019 written warning is more than 24 months old and is therefore extinguished for disciplinary purposes; the Grievor's record is, in effect, clean.
  6. On 24 June 2026 the Employer alleged that the Grievor had falsified a lockout verification record on one occasion.
  7. The Employer conducted no investigation before imposing discipline.
  8. A disciplinary meeting was held that lasted approximately four minutes.
  9. The Grievor had no union representation at the disciplinary meeting.
  10. By letter dated 26 June 2026 the Employer discharged the Grievor, citing "falsification of safety documentation."
  11. The grievance has not yet been filed. The filing deadline under Article 11 (10 working days) is 8 July 2026.

4. Issues

  1. Did the Employer have just cause to discharge the Grievor?
  2. If just cause for some discipline is established, was discharge an excessive penalty warranting substitution of a lesser penalty?
  3. Did the absence of any investigation, the four-minute meeting, and the denial of union representation constitute procedural defects vitiating or mitigating the discipline?
  4. May the Employer rely on the 2019 written warning given the 24-month sunset clause?
  5. What is the appropriate remedy?

5. Argument

A. Preliminary and jurisdictional matters

Time limits and preservation of the grievance. The Union notes that the time limits in Article 11 are stated to be mandatory and that the filing deadline is 8 July 2026. The Union's first priority is to file the grievance within that period. Counsel is instructed to ensure the grievance is filed forthwith and well within the mandatory limit to avoid any preliminary objection based on timeliness. Where mandatory limits are missed, arbitrators retain a discretion under section 48(16) of the Labour Relations Act, 1995 to relieve against time limits where there are reasonable grounds and the responding party is not substantially prejudiced; the Union does not, however, intend to rely on that discretion and will meet the deadline.

Exclusive forum. The essential character of this dispute (the discipline and discharge of an employee under a collective agreement) arises expressly from the interpretation and application of the agreement. Following Weber v. Ontario Hydro, [1995] 2 S.C.R. 929, arbitration is the exclusive forum for the resolution of this dispute, and the arbitrator has full authority to grant the relief sought, including make-whole remedies and damages.

Duty of fair representation. The Union raises this internally: given the mandatory time limits and the approaching 8 July 2026 deadline, prompt filing is essential to avoid exposure under section 74 of the Labour Relations Act, 1995. This note is for the Union's own file management and does not form part of the case against the Employer.

B. The Employer bears the onus and cannot meet it

In a discharge case the onus rests on the Employer to establish, on clear and cogent evidence, both that the misconduct occurred and that it justified the penalty imposed. The governing analytical framework is that articulated in Re United Steelworkers of America, Local 405 v. William Scott & Co., [1977] 1 Can. L.R.B.R. 1 (B.C.L.R.B.), which asks: (1) was there just cause for some discipline; (2) if so, was discharge an excessive response in the circumstances; and (3) if so, what lesser penalty should be substituted.

C. There is no proven cause: the absence of investigation is fatal

The Employer conducted no investigation before discharging a nearly 18-year employee. An allegation of falsification is a serious allegation of dishonesty, and an employer alleging dishonest intent must prove that intent, not merely a discrepancy in a record. A discrepancy or error in a lockout verification record is not, without more, evidence of a deliberate falsification. The Employer has, on the face of the intake, drawn the most serious possible inference (intentional falsification) without conducting any inquiry into whether the record was mistaken, incomplete, or the product of an innocent error. On the present record the Employer cannot discharge its onus of proving deliberate misconduct.

D. Even if some misconduct is proven, discharge is grossly excessive

Should any culpable conduct be established, discharge is a disproportionate response and the arbitrator should substitute a lesser penalty. The mitigating factors here are compelling:

  1. Long service. The Grievor has approximately 18 years of service. Long, clean service is a powerful mitigating factor and weighs heavily against the ultimate penalty of discharge.
  2. A clean disciplinary record. The only prior discipline is a 2019 written warning that is extinguished by the 24-month sunset clause (see Part E below). The Grievor's record is effectively unblemished.
  3. A single, isolated incident. The allegation concerns one occasion only. There is no pattern of misconduct.
  4. Absence of aggravating features. There is no allegation of dishonesty for personal gain, no allegation of harm resulting, and no repetition.

Arbitrators routinely substitute lesser penalties where a long-service employee with a clean record commits an isolated act, particularly where the disciplinary process was flawed. The corrective, rather than punitive, purpose of workplace discipline is not served by ending an 18-year career on the basis of a single unexamined allegation.

E. The prior record is unavailable: the sunset clause

The Collective Agreement provides a 24-month sunset clause. The Grievor's only prior discipline, the 2019 written warning, is well beyond 24 months old and is therefore spent and inadmissible to support the discharge. The Employer cannot rely upon it to establish a disciplinary progression or to characterize the Grievor as anything other than an employee with a clean record.

F. Procedural defects

The discipline is tainted by serious procedural defects, each of which the Union relies upon both as an independent flaw and as a further reason to reduce the penalty:

  1. No investigation. No investigation of any kind was conducted before discharge. An employer that imposes the capital penalty of discharge without inquiry deprives itself of the factual foundation needed to establish just cause and denies the employee any meaningful opportunity to respond.
  2. No union representation. The Grievor had no union representation at the disciplinary meeting. To the extent the Collective Agreement or the parties' practice provides a right to representation at such meetings, its denial is a substantive breach.
  3. A four-minute meeting. A disciplinary meeting lasting approximately four minutes confirms that the decision to discharge was made in advance and that the Grievor was given no genuine opportunity to explain or to be heard. This is the antithesis of a fair process.

6. Remedy

The presumptive remedy for a discharge found to be without just cause is reinstatement with full make-whole compensation. The Union seeks:

  1. A declaration that the Employer did not have just cause to discharge the Grievor;
  2. Reinstatement of the Grievor to his position as Millwright without loss of seniority;
  3. Full back pay from the date of discharge to the date of reinstatement, estimated at approximately $96,000, subject to adjustment as the evidence establishes;
  4. Restoration of all benefits and pension contributions and credits as though the Grievor had remained continuously employed;
  5. Interest on all monetary amounts;
  6. Removal of all reference to the discharge from the Grievor's file.

In the alternative, if any misconduct is established and discharge is nonetheless found excessive, the Union seeks the substitution of a lesser penalty proportionate to the proven conduct, with corresponding compensation for the period of any excess penalty.

7. Order Requested

  1. An order declaring that the Employer did not have just cause to discharge the Grievor;
  2. An order reinstating the Grievor to his position without loss of seniority;
  3. An order for full back pay, benefits, and pension make-whole compensation, with interest;
  4. In the alternative, an order substituting a lesser penalty with appropriate compensation;
  5. An order that the arbitrator remain seized to resolve any dispute concerning the implementation or quantum of the remedy.
