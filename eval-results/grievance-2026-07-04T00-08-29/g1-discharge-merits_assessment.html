SUMMARY OF ASSESSMENT** The recommendation is to **file the grievance immediately** to preserve the mandatory Article 11 time limit (expiring 8 July 2026), and to advance it. Set against 18 years of service and a single, allegedly one-time incident with a stale prior record, the union's position on penalty is arguable-to-strong, though the merits turn on facts about the alleged falsification that are not yet in the file. --- **3. The time limits are stated as MANDATORY and the filing deadline is 8 July 2026. Failure to file within 10 working days risks the grievance being dismissed as untimely, without regard to its merits. While an arbitrator retains discretion under s. 48(16) of the *Labour Relations Act, 1995* to extend time limits where there are reasonable grounds and the opposite party is not substantially prejudiced, that relief is discretionary and should never be relied upon where the deadline can simply be met. **The grievance must be filed immediately.** There is no strategic reason to delay. This issue is not a matter of relative strength; it is an operational imperative. **Issue 2: Procedural defects** The union's strongest ground. The intake records three distinct defects: 1. No investigation was conducted before discipline was imposed. 2. No union representation was afforded at the disciplinary meeting. 3. The discharge meeting lasted four minutes. *Union's best position:* Where a collective agreement or established practice requires an investigation and the opportunity for the employee to respond, the failure to investigate is a serious procedural failing that goes to whether just cause has been established at all, and at minimum bears heavily on the appropriateness of the penalty. A four-minute meeting for the discharge of an 18-year employee, without representation, strongly suggests the decision was made before the grievor was heard. The absence of any investigation into a "one occasion" allegation is particularly significant. *Employer's likely response:* Procedural shortcomings do not automatically void a discharge where just cause exists on the substance; the arbitration itself provides the grievor a full opportunity to answer the allegation. Whether the right to representation was breached depends on what the collective agreement guarantees and whether the meeting was investigatory or purely notificatory. [TO BE CONFIRMED whether Article 11 or another article guarantees representation at disciplinary meetings.] *Assessment:* **Arguable-to-strong.** The cumulative effect of no investigation, no representation, and a four-minute meeting is a compelling narrative of a rushed and unfair process. Whether it defeats the discharge outright or "only" affects penalty depends on the collective agreement's language and the strength of the substantive case. **Issue 3: Just cause challenge (Article 5.02; William Scott framework)** The analysis proceeds under the accepted three-part framework: (1) whether the grievor gave the employer just cause for some discipline; (2) if so, whether discharge was an excessive response; and (3) if so, what alternative penalty is appropriate. *First question (was there cause for some discipline):* Falsification of a safety document, if proven, is serious misconduct because it engages both dishonesty and the integrity of a lockout/tagout safety system. However, "falsification" requires proof of a deliberate, dishonest act, not mere error, oversight, or a good-faith mistake in completing a record. The file does not yet establish the mental element. [TO BE CONFIRMED - what exactly the grievor is alleged to have entered, what the true state was, and whether intent to deceive can be shown.] *Second question (was discharge excessive):* This is where the union's case is potentially strong. Relevant mitigating factors include: 18 years of service; a single alleged occurrence; and a disciplinary record that consists of one stale written warning. Arbitrators weigh long service and an otherwise clean record heavily against summary discharge, particularly where the misconduct is a single incident. *Employer's likely response:* Falsification of safety documentation strikes at the trust essential to the employment relationship and, in a mill environment, at worker safety. The employer will argue that dishonesty in a safety-critical record is a fundamental breach that discharge is proportionate to, regardless of service. *Assessment:* **Arguable on the substance, potentially strong on penalty.** If the employer proves deliberate falsification, discharge may be within the range. If the act was a single lapse, error, or something short of intentional deceit, the case for reinstatement with a lesser penalty is strong given seniority and record. **Issue 4: Prior record challenge** *Union's best position:* The only prior discipline is a 2019 written warning for late reporting, subject to a 24-month sunset clause. On its face that warning would have expired for disciplinary purposes by 2021 and cannot properly be relied upon to support discharge in 2026. If the employer references it in any progressive-discipline rationale, that reliance should be challenged. *Employer's likely response:* The employer may argue the discharge stands on the gravity of the current incident alone and does not depend on the 2019 warning. If so, the prior record is a non-issue for them, which also means the grievor effectively approaches this matter with a clean record. *Assessment:* **Strong on its own terms.** The practical effect is favourable to the union either way: the grievor should be treated as an 18-year employee with no subsisting discipline. **Issue 5: Weber / exclusive forum** The essential character of this dispute (discharge under a just cause provision) arises squarely from the interpretation, application, and administration of the collective agreement. Under the *Weber* line of authority, arbitration is the exclusive forum and the dispute is properly grievable and arbitrable. There is no jurisdictional obstacle to advancing the grievance. This issue is noted for completeness; it does not present a live risk on these facts. **No concern.** **Issue 6: Reinstatement and make-whole** Addressed under Remedy Prospects below. --- **5. EVIDENCE** *In the file:* - The discharge letter dated 26 June 2026. - Basic particulars of service, department, and prior record. *Missing and to be obtained before hearing:* - The lockout verification record alleged to have been falsified, and any records that show the true state of affairs against which falsification is alleged. **Critical.** - The employer's evidence of intent to deceive, as distinct from error. - Any notes, statements, or documents generated at or about the four-minute discharge meeting. - Confirmation of whether any investigation occurred at all, and if none, why. - The collective agreement provisions governing representation at disciplinary meetings and any investigation obligations. - The grievor's full account of the incident, in his own words. - The 2019 warning and confirmation of the sunset clause language and its effect. - Mitigation evidence: the grievor's efforts to find alternative work since 26 June 2026, to support the back pay claim and respond to any mitigation argument. - Confirmation of the $96,000 back pay basis. --- **6. REMEDY PROSPECTS** If the grievance succeeds on penalty, an arbitrator has a broad remedial jurisdiction and could: - **Reinstate** the grievor with full back pay, benefits, and seniority (the remedy sought). This is realistic where discharge is found excessive and the employment relationship is viable, which 18 years of service supports. - **Substitute a lesser penalty** (for example a suspension) where some misconduct is proven but discharge is excessive. Back pay would then be reduced by the length of the substituted suspension. - **Order compensation net of mitigation earnings**, meaning the $96,000 figure is subject to reduction for any income the grievor earned or reasonably could have earned. The mitigation evidence noted above is therefore important. Where the substantive misconduct is proven to be deliberate falsification of a safety record, there is a real risk an arbitrator upholds the discharge notwithstanding service. Reinstatement is a realistic outcome; it is not a certainty. --- **7. CONSIDERATIONS BEYOND THE MERITS** These are legitimate under s. 74 and are stated openly. - **Cost and length:** A discharge arbitration involving a safety-record falsification allegation will require documentary and viva voce evidence and is likely to occupy multiple hearing days, with corresponding cost. - **Interests of the bargaining unit:** Discharge for alleged safety falsification carries broad significance. The unit has an interest in ensuring the employer cannot discharge long-service employees on a summary, uninvestigated basis. Establishing that a proper investigation and representation are required has value beyond this grievor. - **Precedential effect:** A favourable award, or a settlement reinforcing procedural obligations, protects the just cause standard under Article 5.02 and the integrity of the grievance procedure. - **Grievor's circumstances:** An 18-year employee facing discharge with a $96,000 income exposure has a substantial personal stake. These interests weigh in favour of advancing a grievance with genuine merit. --- **8. DUTY OF FAIR REPRESENTATION** The union's obligation under s. 74 of the *Labour Relations Act, 1995* is to represent the grievor without conduct that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith. The decision to advance, settle, or decline may be right or wrong, but it must be honest, considered, and documented. Given the mandatory time limit expiring 8 July 2026, the union must act promptly; allowing the grievance to lapse through inaction on a discharge of this seriousness would create real DFR exposure.
