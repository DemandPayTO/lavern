QUESTION PRESENTED Whether the union should file and advance to arbitration the grievance of Claude Boisvert against his discharge for alleged falsification of a lockout verification record, or instead settle or decline to proceed. A subsidiary and time-sensitive question is whether the grievance must be filed immediately to preserve the union's position under the mandatory time limits in Article 11. --- ## 2. SUMMARY OF ASSESSMENT The recommendation is to **file the grievance immediately** and advance it to arbitration. The employer's case is materially weakened by two significant procedural defects: no investigation was conducted before discipline, and the grievor had no union representation at a discharge meeting that lasted four minutes. Combined with 18 years of largely clean service, a single alleged incident, and a stale prior record, the union's position on both just cause and, in the alternative, penalty is arguable to strong. The immediate priority is preservation of the mandatory filing deadline of **8 July 2026**. --- ## 3. FACTS The following is drawn from the intake data. ANALYSIS ### 4.1 Just cause challenge (Article 5.02) The employer bears the onus of establishing just cause on the *William Scott* framework: whether there was misconduct warranting discipline, whether discharge was an excessive response, and if so, what lesser penalty is appropriate. **Union's best position:** Falsification is a serious allegation that engages honesty and safety, but the employer must prove the falsification actually occurred and was deliberate. A single alleged entry on one occasion, absent any investigation, leaves the employer's proof of intent thin. A genuine error, misunderstanding of the verification procedure, or an incomplete record is not falsification. Even if some misconduct is proven, discharge of an 18-year employee for a single incident is likely excessive given the mitigating factors: long service, an effectively clean record, and the absence of progressive discipline. **Employer's likely response:** Falsification of safety documentation, particularly lockout verification, strikes at the trust essential to a maintenance role and creates a direct risk to worker safety. Employers routinely argue that safety-record falsification is culpable at the highest level and that the bond of trust is broken by a single act, defeating reinstatement. **Assessment:** On the threshold question of whether discharge was warranted, the union's position is **arguable to strong**, primarily because of the mitigating factors and the procedural vacuum around proof of intent. On the narrow question of whether some discipline was warranted if falsification is proven, the union is weaker; safety-document falsification is treated seriously by arbitrators. The realistic centre of gravity is the penalty, not the existence of misconduct. ### 4.2 Procedural defects **Union's best position:** Two defects stand out. First, no investigation was conducted before discipline. An employer that disciplines without investigating deprives itself of the factual foundation just cause requires and forecloses the grievor's opportunity to explain. Second, the grievor had no union representation at the disciplinary meeting. Where a collective agreement or established practice provides for representation, its denial is a substantive breach that arbitrators weigh heavily, sometimes voiding the discipline and often reducing penalty. A four-minute meeting is consistent with a pre-determined outcome rather than a genuine hearing of the grievor's side. **Employer's likely response:** The employer may argue that any procedural right to representation depends on the language of the collective agreement, that a defect can be cured at arbitration through a de novo hearing, and that procedural shortcomings do not by themselves defeat just cause where the underlying misconduct is proven. **Assessment:** **Arguable to strong.** The absence of investigation and of representation are the strongest features of this file. Whether they void the discipline or "merely" inform penalty depends on the collective agreement language **[TO BE CONFIRMED: whether Article 11 or elsewhere provides a right to representation at disciplinary meetings]**. Even at their weakest, these defects materially assist on penalty and on undermining the employer's proof. ### 4.3 Prior record challenge **Union's best position:** The only prior discipline is a 2019 written warning for late reporting, unrelated to falsification or safety. With a 24-month sunset clause, that warning is spent and cannot be relied on to support discharge. The grievor should be treated as having a clean record for disciplinary purposes. **Employer's likely response:** The employer may not rely on the warning at all, or may attempt to reference it as background. Given the sunset clause and the age of the warning, any such reliance is unlikely to succeed. **Assessment:** **Strong.** The prior record does not assist the employer. ### 4.4 Grievance time limits (Article 11) **Union's position:** The time limits are mandatory. The filing deadline is **8 July 2026**. The grievance must be filed before then. There is presently no live limitation issue provided the union acts promptly. **Risk:** If the deadline is missed, the union faces both an arbitrability challenge and, given a discharge of this magnitude, potential DFR exposure. Section 48(16) permits an arbitrator to relieve against time limits on reasonable grounds where the opposite party is not substantially prejudiced, but this is a discretionary remedy and must never be treated as a substitute for timely filing. **Assessment:** No merits issue exists yet; this is an **action item of the highest priority**. ### 4.5 Weber / exclusive forum **Union's position:** The dispute is a discharge under a collective agreement containing a just cause provision. Its essential character arises from the interpretation, application, and administration of the collective agreement. Under the exclusive-jurisdiction principle from *Weber*, this dispute belongs in the grievance and arbitration forum, not the courts. This confirms that arbitration is the correct and only route and forecloses any suggestion that the grievor pursue a civil wrongful-dismissal action. **Assessment:** **Strong / not genuinely in dispute.** This issue supports proceeding to arbitration and counsels against any parallel court process by the grievor. ### 4.6 DFR exposure (s. 74) The union's conduct in handling this grievance is governed by the duty of fair representation. Given a discharge with $96,000 in back pay at stake, an 18-year employee, and evident procedural defects that make the grievance meritorious on its face, a decision to decline or to allow the deadline to lapse would carry **significant DFR risk**. This factor strongly favours filing and giving the grievance genuine consideration on the merits. See section 9 below. --- ## 5. REMEDY PROSPECTS The remedy sought is reinstatement with full back pay, benefits, and seniority. Back pay is estimated at **$96,000 [TO BE CONFIRMED]**. - **Reinstatement with make-whole:** Realistically available if the union succeeds on the threshold just cause question, that is, if the arbitrator is not persuaded the misconduct is proven or finds discharge excessive without a substituted penalty short of termination. The 18-year clean record and procedural defects support reinstatement. - **Substitution of a lesser penalty:** The most likely favourable outcome if some culpable conduct is found. An arbitrator could reinstate with a suspension of some length and reduced back pay. Safety-document falsification, if proven, tends to draw a substantial substituted penalty even where discharge is set aside. - **Back pay:** Subject to the duty to mitigate. The grievor's interim earnings and job-search efforts must be documented **[TO BE CONFIRMED]**. The $96,000 figure is a starting estimate and will move with the hearing date and mitigation. - **Compliance / procedural relief:** The procedural defects strengthen the case for a full remedy but are unlikely to generate a stand-alone monetary award beyond their effect on the discharge itself. --- ## 7. CONSIDERATIONS BEYOND THE MERITS These are legitimate considerations under s. 74 and are stated openly. - **Cost and length:** A contested discharge arbitration involving safety documentation, witness evidence, and procedural argument will be a multi-day matter with corresponding legal and preparation costs. - **Bargaining-unit interest:** Falsification of a lockout verification record engages worker safety, which is a core bargaining-unit interest. The union must be seen to take safety seriously; that said, the more compelling unit interest here is enforcing just cause standards and the right to investigation and representation, which protect every member. This is a case worth defending for its precedential value on procedure. - **Precedential effect:** A favourable award would reinforce the mandatory nature of investigation and representation before discipline. An adverse award on a weakly prepared file could do the opposite. This argues for thorough preparation, not for declining. - **Grievor's circumstances:** An 18-year employee facing loss of career and $96,000 in lost income. The stakes for the individual are high and weigh in favour of proceeding. --- ## 8. DUTY OF FAIR REPRESENTATION The governing standard under s. 74 of the *Labour Relations Act, 1995* is that the union must not act in a manner that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith. The union is entitled to exercise judgment on the merits, but that judgment must be honest, thorough, and non-perfunctory, particularly where a discharge is at issue. **Process followed and to be recorded:** - File reviewed by Jordan Whitfield, Whitfield Labour Law. - Considered: the discharge letter, the grievor's service and record, the sunset clause, the identified procedural defects, and the three witness accounts available. - **Grievor communication [TO BE CONFIRMED]:** the date the grievor was advised of this assessment and of the committee's decision should be documented. Given the mandatory deadline, the grievor should be kept informed in writing at each step. Given the apparent merit and the discharge context, a decision to decline would require careful justification and full documentation to withstand DFR scrutiny. The recommendation below reflects that assessment. --- ## 9. RECOMMENDATION 1. **File the grievance immediately and in any event before 8 July 2026.** This is the paramount, time-critical step. Missing the mandatory deadline would forfeit the grievance and create DFR exposure. 2. **Advance the grievance through Article 11 and refer it to arbitration** if not resolved at the earlier steps. The just cause challenge, supported by the procedural defects, the stale prior record, and 18 years of service, is arguable to strong. 3. **Undertake the evidence-gathering in section 6 promptly**, in particular securing the verification record, confirming the absence of investigation and representation, obtaining the collective agreement's representation language, and taking full statements from Devon Price and Alia Rahman. 4. **Pursue reinstatement with full make-whole relief as the primary remedy**, while preparing, in the alternative, a penalty-substitution argument should some culpable conduct be established. Document the grievor's mitigation efforts. 5. **Remain open to a favourable settlement** (for example, reinstatement with a defined suspension and partial back pay) once the strength of the falsification evidence is known, and report any settlement discussions to the committee.
