Demand Letter — Re: Wrongful Dismissal of Marcus Webb

Dear Sir/Madam:

Re: Wrongful Dismissal of Marcus Webb — Cadence Manufacturing Ltd

We are counsel to Marcus Webb, your former Plant Operations Manager. We have been retained to advance our client's claims arising from the termination of his employment on May 15, 2026. The purpose of this letter is to set out our client's legal position, to demonstrate why your severance offer falls well short of his entitlements, and to invite resolution of this matter without the need for litigation.

We advise that our client will not hesitate to pursue all available remedies should this matter not be resolved on reasonable terms within the timeframe set out below.

Employment Background

Our client was employed by your organization from April 11, 2011 until May 15, 2026 — a period of over 15 years. At the time of his dismissal, he held the senior position of Plant Operations Manager and earned a base salary of $128,000 per year, together with an annual bonus of approximately $15,000, for total annual compensation of approximately $143,000.

Our client was 54 years of age at the date of his termination. He devoted more than a decade and a half of loyal and dedicated service to your organization in a role of significant responsibility.

Termination Facts

On May 15, 2026, your organization terminated our client's employment, citing "restructuring." This was a termination without cause. Your organization has offered our client 15 weeks of severance. For the reasons set out below, that offer is wholly inadequate and does not reflect our client's legal entitlements at common law.

Legal Analysis

1. The Termination Clause Is Invalid and Unenforceable

Your organization will no doubt seek to rely on the termination provision in our client's employment agreement to limit his entitlements to the statutory minimum. That clause reads:

"The Company may terminate your employment at any time without cause upon providing you with the minimum notice or pay in lieu required by the Employment Standards Act, 2000, and nothing more."

The law is clear on this point. In Waksdale v Swegon North America Inc, 2020 ONCA 391, the Court of Appeal for Ontario held that where any part of a termination scheme in an employment agreement violates the Employment Standards Act, 2000, the entire termination provision is void and unenforceable — even the portions that would otherwise be lawful. A termination clause must be read as a whole, and an offending provision cannot be severed to save the balance.

Where a termination clause is found unenforceable, the employee is entitled to reasonable notice at common law: see Machtinger v HOJ Industries Ltd, [1992] 1 SCR 986, in which the Supreme Court of Canada held that clauses purporting to provide less than the statutory minimum are void, and the presumption of reasonable notice at common law is revived.

2. The "At Any Time" Language Renders the Clause Void

The clause purports to reserve to your organization the right to terminate our client's employment "at any time." This language is fatal. It offends the Employment Standards Act, 2000 because there are circumstances in which an employer may not lawfully terminate an employee "at any time" — for example, following the exercise of a statutory leave or the assertion of an ESA right. A clause that grants an unrestricted right to terminate at any time purports to contract out of the Act's protections and is unenforceable, consistent with the Waksdale line of authority. On this basis alone, the termination clause cannot be enforced and our client's common law entitlements are fully engaged.

3. Wrongful Dismissal — Common Law Reasonable Notice

Because the termination clause is unenforceable, our client is entitled to common law reasonable notice, assessed on the well-established factors in Bardal v Globe & Mail, [1960] OJ No 149: the character of the employment, the length of service, the age of the employee, and the availability of similar employment having regard to the experience, training and qualifications of the employee.

Applying these factors to our client:

  1. Length of service: Over 15 years of continuous, dedicated employment.
  2. Age: 54 years of age at termination — a factor that materially lengthens the notice period given the well-documented difficulties older workers face re-entering the labour market.
  3. Character of employment: A senior, managerial position as Plant Operations Manager, carrying significant responsibility.
  4. Availability of similar employment: Senior operations management roles are limited in number and competitive to secure, which will prolong our client's search for comparable re-employment.

Taking these factors together, our client's reasonable notice period falls in the range of 16 to 24 months. Your offer of 15 weeks is a small fraction of that entitlement and falls well short of what the law requires.

4. ESA Statutory Entitlements

Independent of his common law entitlements, our client is entitled at minimum to his statutory entitlements under the Employment Standards Act, 2000:

  1. Statutory notice pay under section 57 of the Act — 8 weeks, being the maximum for an employee with more than eight years of service, equal to approximately $19,692.
  2. Statutory severance pay under section 64 of the Act — our client, having more than five years of service with an employer meeting the statutory payroll threshold, is entitled to severance pay equal to one week per year of service, calculated at approximately $37,154.

These statutory amounts alone — totalling approximately $56,846 — exceed the value of your 15-week offer. That your offer does not even satisfy our client's minimum statutory entitlements underscores its inadequacy.

5. Bonus and Variable Compensation Through the Notice Period

Our client's annual bonus of approximately $15,000 forms an integral part of his compensation and must be included in the assessment of his damages. In Matthews v Ocean Nutrition Canada Ltd, 2020 SCC 26, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that a terminated employee is entitled to damages reflecting all compensation, including bonus and other variable payments, that he would have earned during the reasonable notice period, unless clear and unambiguous language unequivocally removes that entitlement. No such language operates to defeat our client's claim here.

Damages Quantification

Our client's damages, calculated on a common law reasonable notice basis inclusive of bonus, are quantified as follows:

  1. Common law reasonable notice (16–24 months, base salary): $170,667 – $256,000;
  2. Bonus through the notice period, per Matthews: approximately $20,000 – $30,000;
  3. Statutory notice pay (s. 57 ESA): $19,692 (subsumed within the above); and
  4. Statutory severance pay (s. 64 ESA): $37,154 (subsumed within the above).

We reserve our client's right to advance a claim for aggravated or bad faith damages in accordance with Honda Canada Inc v Keays, 2008 SCC 39, should the manner of dismissal or your organization's subsequent conduct warrant it.

This figure represents a reasonable compromise at the mid-point of our client's common law entitlements and reflects his genuine desire to resolve this matter efficiently.

This offer is conditional on prompt resolution and is subject to the execution of a mutually acceptable full and final release.
