Demand Letter — Marcus Webb adv. Cadence Manufacturing Ltd

Dear Sir/Madam:

RE: Wrongful Dismissal of Marcus Webb — Demand for Compensation

We are employment counsel at Whitfield Employment Law and we act on behalf of Marcus Webb in connection with the termination of his employment by Cadence Manufacturing Ltd (the "Company"). We have been retained to secure the full compensation to which our client is entitled at law arising from his dismissal. This letter sets out our client's position and our demand for resolution.

We write to you on a without-prejudice basis, save as to costs.

Employment Background

Our client commenced employment with the Company on April 11, 2011, and was continuously employed for over 15 years until his termination. At the time of dismissal he held the senior position of Plant Operations Manager. He was 54 years of age. His compensation consisted of a base salary of $128,000 per year, together with an annual bonus of $15,000, for total annual compensation of approximately $143,000.

Over more than 15 years of service, our client dedicated a substantial portion of his career to the Company in a senior managerial capacity.

Termination Facts

On May 15, 2026, the Company terminated our client's employment. The stated reason for termination was "restructuring." No allegation of cause has been advanced, and our client's dismissal was therefore a termination without cause. The Company has offered our client 15 weeks of severance. As set out below, that offer falls well short of our client's entitlements and is not acceptable.

Legal Analysis

1. The Termination Clause Is Invalid and Unenforceable

The Company will no doubt seek to rely on the termination clause in our client's employment agreement, which reads:

"The Company may terminate your employment at any time without cause upon providing you with the minimum notice or pay in lieu required by the Employment Standards Act, 2000, and nothing more."

The law is clear on this point: this clause is unenforceable and cannot displace our client's common law entitlement to reasonable notice. Where a termination clause fails to rebut the presumption of reasonable notice in clear and unambiguous language that complies in all respects with the Employment Standards Act, 2000, the clause is void and the employee is entitled to common law reasonable notice: Machtinger v HOJ Industries Ltd, [1992] 1 SCR 986.

2. The "At Any Time" Language Renders the Clause Void

The clause purports to permit the Company to terminate our client "at any time." This language is fatal. It fails to account for the statutory prohibitions against termination in circumstances such as a reprisal or during a protected leave under the Employment Standards Act, 2000. A termination clause that reserves to the employer a right to dismiss at any time, without regard to these statutory protections, is inconsistent with the Act and is therefore unenforceable.

Moreover, under Waksdale v Swegon North America Inc, 2020 ONCA 391, a termination provision must be read as a whole. If any part of the termination scheme violates the Employment Standards Act, 2000, the entire termination clause is void — even the without-cause portion the Company seeks to rely upon, and even if a severability clause is present. The defect in this clause cannot be saved.

3. Wrongful Dismissal — Common Law Reasonable Notice

With the termination clause void, our client is entitled to reasonable notice at common law. The applicable factors are set out in Bardal v Globe & Mail, [1960] OJ No 149: the character of the employment, the length of service, the age of the employee, and the availability of similar employment having regard to the employee's experience, training, and qualifications.

Each of these factors strongly favours a lengthy notice period. Our client was a senior manager (Plant Operations Manager); he had over 15 years of service; he was 54 years of age at termination; and re-employment prospects for a senior operations manager in his mid-fifties are materially constrained. Applying the Bardal factors, our client's reasonable notice period falls in the range of 16 to 24 months.

4. ESA Statutory Entitlements

At an absolute minimum, and quite apart from his common law entitlements, our client is owed his statutory entitlements under the Employment Standards Act, 2000. Pursuant to section 57 of the Act, based on more than 15 years of service, our client is entitled to 8 weeks of statutory notice or pay in lieu, being $19,692. Pursuant to section 64 of the Act, he is also entitled to statutory severance pay of 15 weeks (one week per year of service to a maximum of 26), being approximately $37,154. The Company's offer of 15 weeks in total does not even cover our client's combined statutory notice and severance entitlements, let alone his common law entitlement.

5. Recovery of Bonus Through the Notice Period

Our client's damages must include his annual bonus of $15,000 throughout the reasonable notice period. In Matthews v Ocean Nutrition Canada Ltd, 2020 SCC 26, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that an employee is entitled to compensation for all amounts he would have earned during the notice period, including bonus, unless clear and unambiguous language unequivocally removes that entitlement in circumstances of dismissal. No such language exists here. Our client's bonus forms part of his damages.

Damages Quantification

Our client's entitlements are particularised as follows:

  1. ESA statutory notice pay (8 weeks): $19,692;
  2. ESA statutory severance pay (approximately 15 weeks): $37,154;
  3. Common law reasonable notice (16 to 24 months of base salary): $170,667 to $256,000; and
  4. Bonus through the notice period at $15,000 per annum, to be included in the common law damages calculation.

The Company's offer of 15 weeks' severance is wholly inadequate. It does not satisfy even our client's statutory minimums and bears no relationship to his common law entitlement of 16 to 24 months.

Demand

To resolve this matter without the need for litigation, our client is prepared to accept the sum of $190,000 in full and final settlement of his claims, inclusive of all statutory and common law entitlements. This figure represents a reasonable compromise of our client's position and reflects a discount from the upper end of his damages range.

This offer is open for acceptance for 14 days from the date of this letter.

We note that the applicable limitation period does not expire until May 15, 2028, and our client is fully prepared to litigate this matter to conclusion.
