1. Nature of the Action

This is an action for wrongful dismissal. The Plaintiff, Marcus Webb, was employed by the Defendant, Cadence Manufacturing Ltd, as Plant Operations Manager from April 11, 2011 until his termination on May 15, 2026, a period of approximately 15 years. The Defendant terminated the Plaintiff's employment on the stated basis of a corporate restructuring. The Plaintiff claims damages for wrongful dismissal, including reasonable notice at common law, alleging that the termination provisions in his employment contract are unenforceable. The Plaintiff further claims the value of a lost annual bonus during the notice period.

2. Factual Background

  1. The Plaintiff was hired by the Defendant on April 11, 2011 as Plant Operations Manager.
  2. At the time of termination, the Plaintiff was 54 years of age.
  3. The Plaintiff's compensation consisted of an annual base salary of $128,000 and an annual bonus of $15,000.
  4. The Plaintiff was employed for approximately 15 years of continuous service.
  5. On May 15, 2026, the Defendant terminated the Plaintiff's employment. The stated reason was a corporate restructuring. The termination was not alleged to be for cause.
  6. The Plaintiff's employment agreement contained a termination provision on which the Defendant is expected to rely to limit the Plaintiff's entitlements.

5. Legal Issues

  1. Reasonable Notice (Bardal v. Globe & Mail): Reasonable notice is determined by the character of employment, length of service, age, and availability of similar employment. The Plaintiff, at 54 years old with 15 years' service in a senior managerial role, engages factors that support an upper-range notice period.
  2. Enforceability of Termination Clauses (Waksdale v. Swegon North America): Where a "for cause" termination provision violates the ESA, the entire termination scheme — including the without-cause provision — is unenforceable, regardless of which clause is relied upon. Illegality in any part voids the whole.
  3. Contracting Out (Machtinger; ESA s. 5): An employer cannot contract out of ESA minimum standards; a provision that purports to do so is void, not merely read down.
  4. Bonus / Variable Compensation (Matthews v. Ocean Nutrition Canada): An employee is entitled to damages for lost bonus/incentive compensation during the notice period unless the plan language clearly and unambiguously removes the common law entitlement.
  5. Bad Faith / Moral Damages (Honda v. Keays): Damages may be awarded where the manner of dismissal breaches the duty of good faith. No such conduct is presently pleaded on the facts available.
  6. Mitigation: The Plaintiff must take reasonable steps to seek comparable employment; failure to do so may reduce the award. The onus is on the Defendant to prove failure to mitigate.

7. Settlement Range

  1. Best case: If the termination clause is found void under Waksdale, the notice period is assessed at the upper end (24 months), and the full bonus is included, the gross entitlement approaches $256,000, before mitigation.
  2. Likely range: A realistic outcome, assuming the clause is struck and notice is assessed in the 16–20 month range with the bonus included and modest mitigation, falls in the range of $150,000 to $190,000.
  3. Floor: If the termination clause is upheld, the Plaintiff's minimum entitlement is the ESA statutory package of $56,846 (8 weeks' notice plus severance). This is the baseline below which the Plaintiff will not settle.

Candid assessment of weaknesses: The strength of the entire claim rests substantially on the termination clause being found unenforceable. If the clause survives scrutiny, recovery is limited to the ESA floor. The Defendant will press the mitigation defence, and the Plaintiff's post-termination job search efforts and any interim earnings will materially affect the net figure. The bonus claim depends on the specific plan language, which should be reviewed before mediation to gauge exposure on the Matthews issue.

8. Mediation Objectives

  1. A lump-sum monetary settlement within the likely range identified above.
  2. A positive letter of reference confirming title, tenure, and responsibilities.
  3. Continuation of group benefits for a defined period, or compensation in lieu.
  4. A mutual non-disparagement clause.
  5. Confidentiality on mutually acceptable terms.
  6. Appropriate tax treatment/allocation of the settlement funds where lawful.
  7. Final closure and a full and final release.

9. Practical Considerations

  1. Certainty vs. litigation risk: The binary nature of the Waksdale argument creates meaningful risk for both parties, favouring negotiated resolution.
  2. Cost exposure: Continued litigation carries significant cost and disbursement exposure for both sides, and Rule 49 offers may shift costs.
  3. Emotional toll and closure: The Plaintiff, aged 54 with 15 years of service, has a strong interest in achieving prompt closure and refocusing on re-employment.
  4. Mitigation timeline: Early settlement is advantageous while the notice period is still running and mitigation figures are not yet crystallized.
  5. Reputational considerations: A negotiated reference and non-disparagement terms materially assist the Plaintiff's re-employment prospects, which supports resolution over trial.
