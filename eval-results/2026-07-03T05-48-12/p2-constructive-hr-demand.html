Demand Letter re: Constructive Dismissal of Aisha Osei

Dear Sir/Madam,

Re: Constructive Dismissal of Aisha Osei — Employment with Brightpath Financial Group Inc

We are legal counsel to Aisha Osei (our "client"), who was employed by Brightpath Financial Group Inc as a Senior Client Advisor. We write concerning the circumstances that led to the termination of our client's employment, which we characterize as a constructive dismissal at law. The purpose of this letter is to set out our client's legal entitlements and to invite a prompt and amicable resolution of this matter.

Employment Background

Our client commenced employment with Brightpath Financial Group Inc on September 3, 2019, and remained employed until April 20, 2026 — a period of approximately six years and seven months. At the material time, our client held the position of Senior Client Advisor and earned an annual base salary of $92,000, together with commissions averaging approximately $22,000 per year, for total annual compensation of approximately $114,000. Our client was 41 years of age at the time of termination.

The Circumstances of Termination

Our client suffers from an anxiety disorder. Approximately two weeks after disclosing this disability to Brightpath Financial Group Inc and requesting a gradual return-to-work schedule following a medical leave, our client began to experience a marked and adverse change in the terms and conditions of her employment.

Upon returning from medical leave, our client was moved to a junior desk, was stripped of her established book of clients, and had her commission structure reduced by approximately one half. In addition, our client made a written request for a four-day work week during her first month back at work. That request was denied by email, without any discussion or exploration of alternatives.

These unilateral changes fundamentally altered the essential terms of our client's employment and, taken together, demonstrate a course of conduct that evinced an intention on the part of Brightpath Financial Group Inc to no longer be bound by the employment contract. Our client had no reasonable choice but to treat the employment relationship as at an end.

Legal Analysis

1. Constructive Dismissal

The Supreme Court of Canada in Potter v New Brunswick Legal Aid, 2015 SCC 10, established that constructive dismissal may be found where an employer unilaterally makes a substantial change to an essential term of the employment contract, or where the employer engages in a course of conduct that, viewed objectively, demonstrates an intention no longer to be bound by the contract. Both branches of the Potter test are satisfied here.

The demotion to a junior desk, the removal of our client's book of clients, and the reduction of her commission structure by approximately half each constitute unilateral, substantial changes to the fundamental terms of her employment — most notably her duties and her compensation. A reasonable person in our client's position would have felt that the essential terms of the contract were being substantially altered. Accordingly, our client was constructively dismissed.

2. Wrongful Dismissal — Common Law Reasonable Notice

Having been constructively dismissed without notice or cause, our client is entitled to damages in lieu of reasonable notice at common law. The factors governing reasonable notice were set out in Bardal v Globe & Mail Ltd, [1960] OJ No 149, and include the character of the employment, the length of service, the age of the employee, and the availability of similar employment having regard to the experience, training, and qualifications of the employee.

Applying the Bardal factors — our client's senior role, her six years and seven months of service, her age of 41, and the specialized nature of her position — we estimate the appropriate range of reasonable notice at between 7 and 12 months, representing between $53,667 and $92,000 in damages.

3. ESA Statutory Entitlements

Under the Employment Standards Act, 2000, our client is entitled to statutory notice pay of six weeks pursuant to section 57, being approximately $10,615. As Brightpath Financial Group Inc meets the applicable payroll and service thresholds, our client is further entitled to statutory severance pay pursuant to section 64, being approximately $11,727. These statutory minimums represent a floor and are not a substitute for our client's greater common law entitlement.

4. Human Rights Violation — Discrimination on the Basis of Disability

Our client's anxiety disorder is a disability and a protected ground under the Human Rights Code. The adverse treatment of our client — the demotion, the loss of her clients, and the reduction of her compensation — began only two weeks after she disclosed her disability and requested accommodation. This close temporal connection strongly supports the inference that our client's disability was a factor in the adverse treatment she experienced, contrary to the Code. Our client is entitled to damages for injury to dignity, feelings, and self-respect.

5. Failure to Accommodate Disability

An employer bears a duty to accommodate an employee's disability to the point of undue hardship. Our client made a written request for a four-day work week to facilitate her return to work. Brightpath Financial Group Inc denied that request by email, without any discussion, without exploring alternatives, and without any demonstration that accommodation would have caused undue hardship. This constitutes a clear breach of the duty to accommodate.

6. Bonus, Commission, and Equity Through the Notice Period

In Matthews v Ocean Nutrition Canada Ltd, 2020 SCC 26, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that an employee is entitled to compensation for all amounts that would have been earned during the reasonable notice period, including commissions and bonuses, unless clear and unambiguous contractual language unequivocally removes that entitlement. Our client's commissions, which averaged approximately $22,000 per year, must therefore be included in the calculation of her damages through the notice period.

Damages Quantification

Without limiting our client's entitlements, we quantify her claim as follows:

  1. ESA statutory notice pay (6 weeks): approximately $10,615;
  2. ESA statutory severance pay: approximately $11,727;
  3. Common law reasonable notice (7–12 months), inclusive of base salary and commissions: approximately $53,667 to $92,000;
  4. Damages for lost commissions through the notice period pursuant to Matthews;
  5. Damages for injury to dignity, feelings, and self-respect arising from the breach of the Human Rights Code; and
  6. Damages for the failure to accommodate our client's disability.

Demand

In the interest of resolving this matter without the need for litigation, our client is prepared to accept the sum of $120,000 in full and final settlement of all claims arising from her employment and its termination. This figure reflects a reasonable compromise having regard to our client's common law, statutory, and human rights entitlements, and represents a meaningful saving to your client relative to the costs and risks of litigation.
