1. Overview

The Union's decision not to refer the Complainant's discharge grievance to arbitration was a considered exercise of judgment made after a review of the file, a legal opinion, and a bargaining-unit committee assessment of the merits and prospects of success. That decision falls squarely within the range of conduct protected by section 74 of the Labour Relations Act, 1995, being neither arbitrary, discriminatory, nor made in bad faith, and the application should accordingly be dismissed.

2. Background

The Union is the certified bargaining agent for a bargaining unit that includes the Complainant, who was employed by the Employer as a Warehouse Associate with a seniority date of January 10, 2022. The parties are governed by a collective agreement, the grievance procedure of which is set out at Article 7.

On February 3, 2026, the Complainant was discharged following a positive post-incident drug test administered after a forklift collision. The Employer's stated ground for the discharge was impairment at work, relied upon under a zero-tolerance policy. The Complainant's disciplinary record included a verbal warning for a safety matter in 2024 and a written warning arising from a near-miss in 2025.

The Union filed a grievance on the Complainant's behalf on February 6, 2026 (grievance #2026-419-008), challenging the discharge. The grievance was advanced through the grievance procedure to Step 2, the final step. The Employer delivered its Step 2 response on March 12, 2026, maintaining the discharge.

Following the Step 2 response, the Union obtained a legal opinion assessing the prospects of success at arbitration, which were assessed as poor. After a review by a bargaining-unit committee, the Union declined to refer the grievance to arbitration. The Complainant subsequently filed this application under section 74.

3. The Union's Process

The Union submits that its handling of the grievance reflects a diligent, honest, and considered process, and pleads the following facts:

  1. The Union filed the grievance promptly, within days of the discharge, and advanced it through the grievance procedure to the final step, making the Complainant's case to the Employer at each step.
  2. Following receipt of the Employer's Step 2 response, the Union obtained a legal opinion assessing the merits of the grievance and the likelihood of success at arbitration. That opinion assessed the prospects as poor.
  3. In forming its assessment, the Union weighed the relevant considerations, including: the nature of the misconduct alleged (a positive post-incident drug test following a forklift collision in a safety-sensitive workplace); the applicable arbitral jurisprudence governing discharges arising from positive drug tests, workplace drug and alcohol policies, and impairment in safety-sensitive roles; the Complainant's prior disciplinary record; and the costs and likelihood of success of proceeding to arbitration.
  4. The decision not to refer the grievance to arbitration was not made by a single official acting alone. It was reviewed and made by a bargaining-unit committee, following the legal opinion.

4. The Law

Section 74 of the Labour Relations Act, 1995 provides that a trade union shall not act in a manner that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith in the representation of employees in the bargaining unit. The Union relies on the following well-settled principles:

  1. The Union is entitled to exercise judgment in deciding whether to advance or settle a grievance, and to weigh the merits of the grievance, the cost of proceeding, the likelihood of success, and the interests of the bargaining unit as a whole.
  2. The Board does not sit in appeal of the Union's judgment. It is not the Board's role to substitute its own view of the merits of the grievance for that of the Union.
  3. A Union is entitled to be wrong in its assessment, provided the decision was reached through a process that was considered, informed, and honest.
  4. Mere negligence, error, or a difference of opinion as to the merits does not constitute a breach of the duty of fair representation.
  5. The onus rests on the Complainant to establish, on the material pleaded, conduct that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith.

The Union submits that its decision here — grounded in a legal opinion and a committee review, and directed to the merits and prospects of the grievance — is the very kind of judgment that section 74 is intended to protect.

  2. Allegation that the decision was made to save money. The Union denies that cost was the operative or determining reason for its decision. Cost is a legitimate consideration that a union is entitled to weigh among the interests of the bargaining unit as a whole, and its consideration does not, without more, establish a breach of section 74. Here, the determinative factor was the assessed weakness of the grievance on its merits.
