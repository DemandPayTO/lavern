ANALYSIS** **4.1 Just Cause Challenge (Article 5.02)** Under the *William Scott* framework, an arbitrator asks: (1) was there just cause for some discipline; (2) if so, was discharge excessive; and (3) if excessive, what substitute is appropriate. The burden rests on the employer to prove the misconduct on the balance of probabilities, and where the allegation is falsification, an allegation of dishonesty, arbitrators generally require clear and cogent evidence. *Union's best position:* The employer must prove that the grievor knowingly and deliberately falsified the record. Falsification requires intent to deceive; error, oversight, or a good-faith discrepancy in how a verification was recorded is not falsification. On the mitigation stage, even if some misconduct is established, discharge is a severe response to a single occurrence by an employee with 18 years of service and no active discipline. Long service, an otherwise clean record, and a single incident are classic mitigating factors weighing toward substitution of a lesser penalty. *Employer's likely response:* Falsification of safety documentation, particularly a lockout verification, goes to the integrity of the safety system and the trust relationship; arbitrators have upheld discharge for safety-record dishonesty where the breach is serious. The employer will characterize this as a bond-of-trust case in a hazardous industrial setting. *Assessment:* The liability question turns on evidence of intent, which is TO BE CONFIRMED. On the mitigation question, the union's position is strong: a single incident against 18 years of service with no active record is fertile ground for substitution of penalty even if some culpability is found. **4.2 Procedural Defects** *Union's best position:* No investigation was conducted before discipline. Many collective agreements and arbitral norms require a fair investigation, including an opportunity for the employee to respond, before serious discipline. A four-minute discharge meeting is consistent with a predetermined outcome and the absence of any genuine investigation. Where the agreement or established practice requires investigation or representation, the failure may render the discipline procedurally defective and, depending on the language, may void or weaken it. *Employer's likely response:* Procedural shortcomings do not automatically void discipline; absent mandatory language in the agreement, an arbitrator can consider the merits and may cure procedural defects at the hearing. The employer will argue the facts speak for themselves. *Assessment:* Arguable to strong, depending on the agreement's language. Even where a defect does not void discipline, the complete absence of an investigation materially undermines the reliability of the employer's factual case and supports the mitigation argument. If the collective agreement confers a right to representation at disciplinary meetings, its denial is a substantive breach. Even absent express language, the denial reinforces the procedural unfairness and the sense of a foregone conclusion. *Employer's likely response:* Absent an express contractual right, there is no free-standing entitlement to representation at every meeting; the remedy for any breach is limited. *Assessment:* Arguable, elevated to strong if the agreement contains a representation right. [Confirm agreement language and whether representation was requested and refused.] **4.4 Prior Record Challenge** *Union's best position:* The only prior discipline is a 2019 written warning subject to a 24-month sunset clause. It is long expired and cannot be relied upon to support progressive discipline or to aggravate penalty. The grievor's disciplinary record should be treated as clean for arbitral purposes. *Employer's likely response:* The employer is unlikely to be able to rely on the 2019 warning given the sunset clause and is likely to argue that the current misconduct is serious enough to warrant discharge on its own, regardless of record. *Assessment:* Strong on the narrow point that the prior warning cannot be used. This strengthens the mitigation analysis under *William Scott*. **4.5 Grievance Time Limits (Article 11)** *Union's best position:* Time limits are stated as MANDATORY; the filing deadline is 2026-07-08. The grievance must be filed on or before that date to preserve the claim. Section 48(16) of the *Labour Relations Act, 1995* permits an arbitrator to relieve against time limits where there are reasonable grounds and no substantial prejudice, but this relief is discretionary and should not be relied upon where the deadline can simply be met. *Employer's likely response:* If the grievance is filed late, the employer will raise the mandatory time limit as a complete bar and will resist s. 48(16) relief. *Assessment:* This is not a merits issue but a critical procedural imperative. File before 2026-07-08. Missing the deadline converts a strong grievance into a fight over discretionary relief and creates DFR exposure for the union. **4.6 Weber / Exclusive Forum** *Union's best position:* The dispute, a discharge under a collective agreement, falls squarely within the exclusive arbitral jurisdiction under *Weber*. The essential character of the dispute arises from the collective agreement, and arbitration is the proper and exclusive forum. This supports proceeding through the grievance procedure rather than any external avenue. *Employer's likely response:* The employer is unlikely to contest arbitral jurisdiction over a discharge grievance. *Assessment:* Strong and effectively uncontroversial. It confirms that the union's own process is the correct route and that no parallel forum is available to the grievor, which reinforces the union's DFR obligations. --- **5. REMEDY PROSPECTS** The remedy sought is reinstatement with full back pay, benefits, and seniority; the back-pay estimate is $96,000. If the union succeeds on liability, an arbitrator can order reinstatement with full make-whole relief: back pay net of interim earnings (the duty to mitigate applies), restoration of seniority, and benefits. Given 18 years of service, no active record, and a single incident, reinstatement is a realistic outcome. If the arbitrator finds some culpability but that discharge was excessive, the likely result is substitution of a lesser penalty (for example, a suspension) with a corresponding reduction in back pay. This is a realistic middle outcome the committee should anticipate. The $96,000 back-pay figure is an estimate and will be affected by mitigation earnings and the length of time to hearing. --- **7. CONSIDERATIONS BEYOND THE MERITS** These are legitimate considerations under s. 74. - *Cost and length:* A contested discharge arbitration involving safety documentation will require document production and multiple witnesses and will be time-consuming and costly. - *Bargaining unit interest:* Falsification of safety records and lockout verification is a matter of collective concern in an industrial mill; the manner of discipline (no investigation, no representation, a four-minute meeting) is of significant institutional interest. Allowing such a process to stand unchallenged would set an unfavourable precedent for how the employer disciplines. - *Precedential effect:* A favourable award would reinforce investigation and representation expectations and the operation of the sunset clause. An adverse award on safety-record integrity could be cited against the unit in future. - *Grievor's circumstances:* 18 years of service and a discharge that ends a long-tenured career weigh strongly in favour of advancing the grievance. --- **8. DUTY OF FAIR REPRESENTATION** Under s. 74 of the *Labour Relations Act, 1995*, the union must not act in a manner that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith. The standard requires a considered, honest, non-arbitrary decision, not a correct one. This case carries elevated DFR exposure for two reasons: it is a discharge affecting a long-service member, and the mandatory filing deadline of 2026-07-08 is imminent. Allowing the deadline to lapse without a documented decision would risk an arbitrary or negligent handling finding. The prudent course is to file to preserve the grievance and then complete the merits review.
