1. Overview

The Union's decision not to refer the Grievor's discharge grievance to arbitration was a considered, honest exercise of judgment made after a merits assessment, a written legal opinion, and a review by a bargaining-unit committee. The application discloses no conduct that was arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith within the meaning of section 74, and it should be dismissed.

2. Background

The Union is the certified bargaining agent for a unit of employees of the Employer, and the terms of employment are governed by a collective agreement that provides for a grievance procedure under Article 7. The Grievor, a Warehouse Associate with a seniority date of 10 January 2022, was discharged on 3 February 2026.

The discharge followed a positive post-incident drug test administered after a forklift collision. The Employer's stated ground for discharge was impairment at work, relying on its zero-tolerance policy. The Grievor's disciplinary record included a verbal warning in 2024 relating to safety and a written warning in 2025 relating to a near-miss.

The Union filed a grievance on 6 February 2026 (Grievance No. 2026-419-008) and advanced it through the grievance procedure to Step 2, the final internal step. The Employer delivered its Step 2 response on 12 March 2026. After obtaining a legal opinion and conducting a committee review, the Union declined to refer the grievance to arbitration. The Grievor then filed this application under section 74.

3. The Union's Process

The Union's handling of the grievance reflects a documented and deliberate process, not inaction. The following steps were taken and are supported by the Union's records:

  1. The Union filed the grievance promptly, within days of the discharge, and advanced it through the grievance procedure to Step 2 (final).
  2. The Union assessed the merits of the grievance, including the nature of the Employer's grounds (a positive post-incident drug test following a forklift collision and reliance on a zero-tolerance policy) and the Grievor's prior disciplinary record (a 2024 safety-related verbal warning and a 2025 near-miss written warning).
  3. The Union obtained a written legal opinion, which assessed the prospects of success at arbitration as poor.
  4. The grievance, the merits assessment, and the legal opinion were placed before a bargaining-unit committee, which reviewed the file and considered whether to refer the matter to arbitration.
  5. Following that review, the Union decided not to refer the grievance to arbitration, weighing the merits, the likelihood of success, and the interests of the bargaining unit as a whole.
  6. The Union communicated with the Grievor in writing on four separate occasions during the course of the grievance, keeping the Grievor informed of the status of the matter and the basis for the decisions taken.

The Union relies on the following contemporaneous documents to establish this process: the grievance filing, the committee minutes recording the review, the written legal opinion, and the four written updates provided to the Grievor.

4. The Law

Section 74 of the Labour Relations Act, 1995 provides that a union shall not act in a manner that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith in representing employees in the bargaining unit. The standard is well settled:

  1. The Board does not sit in appeal of the merits of a union's decision. The question is not whether the Union was right, but whether its decision-making process was arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith.
  2. A union is entitled to weigh the merits of a grievance, the likelihood of success, the cost of proceeding, and the interests of the bargaining unit as a whole. A union may decline to refer even a discharge grievance to arbitration where, after a considered assessment, it concludes the prospects of success are poor.
  3. Mere negligence, error in judgment, or a decision that later proves to have been mistaken does not, without more, breach the duty. A union is entitled to be wrong, provided its decision was made honestly and after genuine consideration.
  4. The onus rests on the complainant to establish, on the material pleaded, conduct meeting the statutory threshold.

5. Response to the Specific Allegations

  1. Allegation that the Union "did nothing" and settled cheap. The Union denies this allegation. The record establishes that the Union filed the grievance promptly, advanced it to the final internal step, obtained a written legal opinion, conducted a bargaining-unit committee review, and provided the Grievor with four written updates. This is the opposite of inaction. The decision not to proceed to arbitration was the product of a considered assessment of poor prospects of success, which is a legitimate exercise of the Union's judgment.

  3. Allegation that the decision was made to save money. The Union denies that the decision was made improperly to save money. For completeness, the Union notes that the underlying grievance raised issues going to just cause for the discharge, the reasonableness and enforceability of the Employer's zero-tolerance policy, the treatment of the Grievor's prior disciplinary record, and the availability of reinstatement and make-whole relief. The Grievor's underlying complaints concerning the merits of the discharge fall within the exclusive arbitral forum and are not matters for the Board to decide on this application.
