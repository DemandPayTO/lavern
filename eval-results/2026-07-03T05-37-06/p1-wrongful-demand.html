RE: Wrongful Dismissal of Marcus Webb by Cadence Manufacturing Ltd

We write regarding the unlawful termination of our client's employment and to place your client on notice of our client's claims. We invite you to review this correspondence carefully, as it sets out both the strength of our client's position and the consequences of your client's failure to resolve this matter promptly.

Employment Background

Our client commenced employment with the Company on April 11, 2011, and was continuously employed for over fifteen years in a role of significant responsibility. At the time of termination, our client was 54 years of age and earned an annual base salary of $128,000, together with an annual bonus of $15,000, for total annual compensation of approximately $143,000.

The Termination

On May 15, 2026, the Company terminated our client's employment on a without-cause basis, citing "restructuring." In connection with the termination, your client offered our client a mere 15 weeks of severance. As set out below, this offer falls well short of our client's legal entitlements and is not one our client is prepared to accept.

Legal Analysis

1. The Termination Clause Is Invalid and Unenforceable

Your client will no doubt seek to rely on the termination provision in our client's employment agreement to limit its liability. That reliance is misplaced. The clause reads:

"The Company may terminate your employment at any time without cause upon providing you with the minimum notice or pay in lieu required by the Employment Standards Act, 2000, and nothing more."

The law is clear on this point. A termination clause that fails to comply with the Employment Standards Act, 2000 ("ESA") in any respect is void in its entirety and unenforceable, and the employee is entitled to common law reasonable notice: see Machtinger v HOJ Industries Ltd, [1992] 1 SCR 986. The clause above purports to limit our client to "the minimum notice or pay in lieu" under the ESA and "nothing more" — language that offends the ESA by, among other things, failing to preserve our client's statutory entitlement to severance pay and to the continuation of benefits during the statutory notice period.

2. The "At Any Time" Language Renders the Clause Void (Waksdale)

The clause is independently unenforceable because it purports to reserve to the Company the right to terminate "at any time." In Waksdale v Swegon North America Inc, 2020 ONCA 391, the Court of Appeal for Ontario confirmed that a termination provision must be read as a whole, and that if any part of it contravenes the ESA, the entire termination scheme is void — even the portions on which the employer seeks to rely. Language purporting to permit termination "at any time" and "without cause" on ESA minimums has been repeatedly found to be inconsistent with the statutory protections afforded to employees. Your client cannot rely selectively on the offending clause to defeat our client's common law rights. The clause is void, and our client's entitlement to reasonable notice at common law is fully restored.

3. Wrongful Dismissal — Common Law Reasonable Notice

With the termination clause struck, our client is entitled to reasonable notice of termination at common law. The governing factors are set out in Bardal v Globe & Mail, [1960] OJ No 149: the character of the employment, the length of service, the age of the employee, and the availability of similar employment having regard to the employee's experience, training, and qualifications. Applying those factors here:

  1. Length of service: over 15 years of continuous service;
  2. Age: 54 years at termination, a stage of career at which re-employment is materially more difficult;
  3. Character of employment: a senior, managerial role (Plant Operations Manager) carrying significant responsibility; and
  4. Availability of comparable employment: limited, given the specialised and senior nature of the position and prevailing labour market conditions.

On a proper application of the Bardal factors, our client's reasonable notice period falls in the range of 16 to 24 months.

4. ESA Statutory Entitlements

Independent of and in addition to our client's common law entitlements, the ESA guarantees minimum entitlements that cannot be contracted out of. Pursuant to section 57 of the ESA, our client is entitled to 8 weeks of statutory notice or pay in lieu, being approximately $19,692. Pursuant to section 64 of the ESA, given our client's length of service and the size of the Company's payroll, our client is also entitled to statutory severance pay of approximately $37,154. Your client's offer of 15 weeks does not even discharge the combined statutory minimum obligations, let alone the common law entitlement.

5. Bonus Through the Notice Period

Our client's damages must also include the value of the annual bonus of $15,000 that our client would have earned throughout the reasonable notice period. In Matthews v Ocean Nutrition Canada Ltd, 2020 SCC 26, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that an employee is entitled to damages for the loss of bonus and other compensation that would have been earned during the notice period, unless clear and unambiguous language unequivocally removes that entitlement. No such language exists here, and in any event any such language contained within the void termination scheme cannot assist your client.

Damages Quantification

Our client's entitlements are particularised as follows:

  1. ESA statutory notice pay (8 weeks): $19,692;
  2. ESA statutory severance pay: $37,154;
  3. Common law reasonable notice (16–24 months of base salary): $170,667 to $256,000; and
  4. Loss of bonus through the notice period, calculated at $15,000 per annum over the notice period.

We note that the statutory entitlements are the minimum floor and are subsumed within, not additional to, the larger common law figure.
