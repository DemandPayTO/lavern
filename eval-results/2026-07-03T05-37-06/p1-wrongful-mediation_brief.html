1. Nature of the Action

This is an action for wrongful dismissal. The Plaintiff, Marcus Webb, was employed by the Defendant, Cadence Manufacturing Ltd, as Plant Operations Manager from April 11, 2011 until the termination of his employment on May 15, 2026, a period of approximately 15 years. The Plaintiff's employment was terminated on a without-cause basis, the Defendant citing restructuring. The Plaintiff claims damages for wrongful dismissal, including pay in lieu of reasonable notice at common law, and asserts that the termination provisions in his employment contract are unenforceable. The Plaintiff further claims his lost bonus entitlement over the notice period.

2. Factual Background

  1. The Plaintiff commenced employment with the Defendant on April 11, 2011.
  2. At the time of termination, the Plaintiff held the position of Plant Operations Manager, a senior management role.
  3. The Plaintiff's compensation consisted of an annual base salary of $128,000, together with an annual bonus of $15,000.
  4. The Plaintiff was 54 years of age at the time of termination.
  5. On May 15, 2026, the Defendant terminated the Plaintiff's employment on a without-cause basis, citing a restructuring.
  6. The Plaintiff had approximately 15 years of continuous service at the date of termination.

Further particulars concerning the Plaintiff's efforts to mitigate his damages and any re-employment will be provided as they become available and are relevant to the reasonable notice assessment.

3. Issues in Dispute

  1. Enforceability of the termination clause.

    Plaintiff's position: The termination provision in the employment contract is unenforceable and cannot limit the Plaintiff to less than common law reasonable notice.

    Anticipated defence position: The termination clause is valid, clearly drafted, and limits the Plaintiff's entitlement to his statutory minimums under the Employment Standards Act, 2000 ("ESA").

  2. Waksdale / "at any time" invalidity.

    Plaintiff's position: The for-cause and without-cause provisions must be read together; if any part offends the ESA, the entire termination scheme is void, and any "at any time" or discretionary language further undermines enforceability.

    Anticipated defence position: The clauses comply with the ESA, are severable, and remain enforceable.

  3. Wrongful dismissal / reasonable notice.

    Plaintiff's position: The Plaintiff is entitled to common law reasonable notice in the range of 16 to 24 months, having regard to his age, length of service, senior managerial character of employment, and the availability of comparable employment.

    Anticipated defence position: Notice, if the contract fails, should fall at the lower end of the range; the Defendant will point to any re-employment and mitigation.

  4. ESA severance entitlement.

    Plaintiff's position: The Plaintiff is entitled to statutory notice and severance pay as a baseline, which cannot be contracted out of.

    Anticipated defence position: The Defendant does not dispute ESA minimums (subject to confirmation of payroll size and severance eligibility).

  5. Bonus entitlement over the notice period (Matthews).

    Plaintiff's position: The Plaintiff's $15,000 bonus is an integral part of his compensation and must be included in his damages over the notice period unless clear, unambiguous contractual language removes it.

    Anticipated defence position: The bonus was discretionary and/or subject to an active-employment requirement that excludes it from notice-period damages.

5. Legal Issues

  1. Reasonable notice — Bardal v. Globe & Mail Ltd.

    Reasonable notice is assessed on the character of employment, length of service, age, and availability of similar employment. Here, the Plaintiff's senior managerial role, approximately 15 years of service, and age of 54 support a notice period at the higher end of the range.

  2. Termination clause enforceability — Waksdale v. Swegon North America Inc.

    Where a for-cause provision violates the ESA, the entire termination scheme — including an otherwise valid without-cause clause — is void. Ancillary "at any time" and discretionary language, and any provision purporting to contract below ESA minimums, renders the clause unenforceable, reviving common law notice.

  3. Good faith / manner of dismissal — Honda Canada Inc. v. Keays.

    Aggravated or moral damages may be available where the manner of dismissal is untruthful, misleading, or unduly insensitive. No such facts are presently pleaded; this remains a placeholder pending further particulars.

  4. Bonus over notice period — Matthews v. Ocean Nutrition Canada Ltd.

    The test is (1) whether the bonus is an integral part of compensation, and (2) whether contractual language unambiguously removes it during the notice period. A boilerplate "active employment" clause is generally insufficient. The Plaintiff's regular $15,000 bonus supports inclusion.

  5. ESA baseline.

    The ESA provides for up to 8 weeks' notice and severance pay (for qualifying employers with payroll of $2.5 million or more) based on years of service. These minimums cannot be waived and form the floor of any resolution.

7. Settlement Range

The following assessment is candid and reflects both the strengths and the litigation risks of the Plaintiff's case.

  1. Best case: If the termination clause is struck under Waksdale, notice is fixed at 24 months, and the bonus is included, gross damages approach $256,000 in base plus approximately $30,000 in bonus (approximately $286,000), before mitigation. This outcome assumes minimal mitigation and every issue resolving in the Plaintiff's favour.
  2. Likely range: A realistic negotiated outcome, assuming the termination clause is vulnerable and notice lands in the 18–20 month range with some bonus recovery and some mitigation, falls in the range of $150,000 to $200,000. The Plaintiff's pleaded claim of $190,000 sits at the upper end of this band.
  3. Floor (minimum acceptable): The ESA entitlements of $56,846 ($19,692 notice + $37,154 severance) represent the absolute baseline that cannot be contracted out of and must be paid regardless of the enforceability arguments.

8. Mediation Objectives

  1. A monetary settlement within the likely range identified above, structured tax-efficiently where possible (e.g., allocation to retiring allowance / RRSP roll-over).
  2. A positive letter of reference confirming the Plaintiff's role, tenure, and good performance.
  3. Continuation of benefits for a defined period, or compensation in lieu.
  4. Mutual non-disparagement terms.
  5. Confirmation of a neutral, without-cause reason for departure consistent with the stated restructuring.
  6. Full and final resolution with mutual releases.

9. Practical Considerations

  1. Contractual uncertainty: The strength of the Waksdale argument depends on the precise wording of the termination clause. If the clause is compliant, the Plaintiff's recovery may be limited to ESA minimums — a material risk that favours negotiated resolution.
  2. Mitigation: As a 54-year-old senior manager, the Plaintiff faces a genuinely limited market for comparable roles, supporting a longer notice period; however, any re-employment income will reduce common law damages.
  3. Bonus characterisation: Recovery of the bonus over the notice period turns on the plan language, which introduces uncertainty into the higher end of the claim.
  4. Cost exposure: Proceeding to trial carries significant cost risk to both parties; early resolution avoids that exposure and the delay inherent in the Toronto trial list.
  5. Emotional toll and closure: The Plaintiff, after approximately 15 years of service, has a strong interest in achieving certainty, financial security, and closure rather than protracted litigation.
